\title{Spectral Analysis of U.S.\ Equity Markets:\\
Factor Structure, Sectoral Geometry, and Market Efficiency}

\author{Damon H.\,T.\ Cheung}
% \email{damoncht@umich.edu}
\institute{University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI 48109}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We apply spectral and data-driven methods to the daily returns of 20 large-cap
U.S.\ equities (2020--2026) to characterise the mathematical structure of the stock
market and test whether that structure enables temporal prediction.
Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) reveals that the return cross-section is
approximately rank-4: a single \emph{market factor} captures 44.6\% of variance and
correlates at $r = 0.883$ with the S\&P~500 index, while a random-matrix noise
threshold confirms that only four components represent genuine signal.
Classical Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) and Spectral Clustering then show that
the underlying structure is \emph{sectoral}: without any sector labels, the
algorithms recover the four known sectors (Technology, Finance, Healthcare, Energy)
with perfect accuracy.
Finally, Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) reveals why temporal prediction remains
elusive: all DMD eigenvalues lie deep inside the unit circle, indicating modes that
decay within a single trading day, and a trained model performs \emph{worse} than
simply predicting no change.
These results reconcile an apparent paradox—the market is genuinely low-dimensional
in \emph{cross-section} yet genuinely unpredictable in \emph{time}—and provide a
direct mathematical signature of the Efficient Market Hypothesis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The U.S.\ equity market, with more than \$40 trillion in market capitalisation and
thousands of professional analysts, is one of the most intensively studied dynamical
systems in the world—and one of the most difficult to predict \citep{fama1970efficient}.
This difficulty might suggest a maximally complex system, yet practitioners have long
known that most stocks move together: when the broad market falls, individual
stocks tend to fall with it regardless of their fundamentals.

This co-movement raises a precise mathematical question: how many independent
directions of variation does the equity return matrix truly possess?
If that number is small, the market is \emph{low-dimensional} in a linear-algebraic
sense, and a suite of spectral and data-driven methods—SVD, MDS, Spectral Clustering,
and DMD—become directly applicable.

We analyse daily closing returns for 20 large-cap U.S.\ stocks spanning four sectors
over the period 2020--2026.
Our central finding is that \emph{low-dimensional cross-sectional structure and
temporal unpredictability are not contradictory; they are two sides of the same
efficient-market coin}.
The structure concerns \emph{how stocks co-move with each other} on a given day;
unpredictability concerns \emph{whether tomorrow can be forecast from today}.
SVD reveals the former; DMD falsifies the latter.

\section{Data and Methods}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Data.}
We use daily adjusted closing prices from Yahoo Finance for 20 large-cap U.S.\ stocks,
five from each of four sectors:
\textit{Technology} (AAPL, MSFT, GOOGL, AMZN, META),
\textit{Finance} (JPM, BAC, GS, MS, C),
\textit{Healthcare} (JNJ, UNH, PFE, MRK, ABBV), and
\textit{Energy} (XOM, CVX, COP, SLB, EOG),
plus the S\&P~500 index (used as an external benchmark only).
The period is 2 January 2020 to 24 April 2026 (1{,}585 trading days).
We compute daily log-returns and standardise each stock to zero mean and unit
variance, yielding a matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N}$ with
$T = 1{,}585$ and $N = 20$.

\paragraph{SVD and Random-Matrix Baseline.}
We compute the economy SVD $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$
and interpret the singular values via the Eckart--Young theorem \citep{eckart1936approximation}:
the best rank-$k$ approximation retains the top-$k$ singular values.
To distinguish genuine factors from finite-sample noise we apply the
Mar\v{c}enko--Pastur law \citep{marchenko1967distribution}: for an $i.i.d.$
Gaussian matrix with aspect ratio $q = N/T$, eigenvalues of the sample correlation
matrix are bounded by $\lambda_{\pm} = (1 \pm \sqrt{q})^2$.
Eigenvalues exceeding $\lambda_+ \approx 1.30$ indicate statistically real structure.

\paragraph{Correlation, MDS, and Spectral Clustering.}
We build the $20 \times 20$ Pearson correlation matrix $\mathbf{C}$ of returns.
Classical MDS embeds the stocks in $\mathbb{R}^2$ using the
correlation-based distance $d_{ij} = \sqrt{2(1-r_{ij})}$, via double-centering and
eigendecomposition \citep{kutz2016dynamic}.
Spectral clustering \citep{luxburg2007tutorial} with $k=4$ uses
$\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{1})$ as an affinity matrix and is evaluated against
the known sector labels using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI).

\paragraph{Dynamic Mode Decomposition.}
We model the market as a linear discrete-time system $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_t$
and recover $\mathbf{A}$ via DMD \citep{kutz2016dynamic}.
Training uses 2020--2022 (755 days); testing uses 2023--2026 (830 days).
The DMD operator is $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{X}_2 \mathbf{X}_1^\dagger$ estimated
through a rank-$r$ truncated SVD of the snapshot matrix $\mathbf{X}_1$.
We evaluate one-step-ahead forecasts against a zero baseline (predict no change).

\section{Results}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{SVD Reveals a Low-Rank Cross-Sectional Structure}

Figure~\ref{fig:svd}a shows the scree plot of normalised singular values.
PC1 alone captures 44.6\% of total variance; the top three components together
account for 67.9\%, and the top five for 78.3\%.
The Mar\v{c}enko--Pastur bound $\lambda_+ = 1.24$ is exceeded by exactly four
eigenvalues (Fig.~\ref{fig:svd}a, vertical dotted line), confirming that only
four components carry statistically genuine signal; the remaining 16 are
indistinguishable from noise.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig1_svd.pdf}
  \caption{SVD of the standardised return matrix $\mathbf{X}$.
  \textbf{(a)} Scree plot with cumulative variance and the Marchenko--Pastur noise
  floor (dotted vertical line at three factors).
  \textbf{(b)} Stock loadings on PC1 vs.\ PC2.
  All 20 stocks have positive PC1 loadings (market factor);
  PC2 separates Energy/Finance from Technology.}
  \label{fig:svd}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:svd}b shows the stock loadings $v_1^{(i)}$ and $v_2^{(i)}$ for each
stock.
Every stock carries a positive PC1 loading—reflecting the market-wide co-movement—
and the PC1 projection correlates at $r = 0.883$ with the external S\&P~500 index.
This is the \emph{market factor} identified by factor models in the finance
literature \citep{fama1993common}.
Importantly, however, the 44.6\% figure is inflated by our stock selection:
all 20 stocks are large-cap S\&P~500 constituents, sharing institutional
ownership and index-linked flows.
A more heterogeneous universe (small-caps, bonds, commodities) would yield a
substantially smaller PC1.

\subsection{Classical MDS and Spectral Clustering Recover Sector Structure}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig2_structure.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{(a)} Classical MDS embedding using correlation-based distance.
  Stocks cluster by sector without sector labels being supplied to the algorithm.
  \textbf{(b)} Pearson return correlation matrix sorted by sector, with
  sector boundaries marked.
  Within-sector blocks are consistently warm (high correlation);
  cross-sector blocks are cool.}
  \label{fig:structure}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:structure}a shows the 2-D MDS embedding.
The four sectors separate cleanly: Technology stocks cluster in the upper region,
Energy stocks to the lower left, and Finance and Healthcare in between.
This is a purely data-driven result; no sector information was provided to the
algorithm.

Spectral clustering with $k=4$ reproduces the known sector partition exactly,
achieving ARI~$= 1.00$.
This perfect recovery is supported by Figure~\ref{fig:structure}b: the
sector-sorted correlation matrix displays a pronounced block-diagonal
structure, with within-sector correlations substantially exceeding cross-sector ones.
The correlation heatmap mirrors the MDS geometry: Technology--Technology pairs are
the most tightly correlated, while Energy--Technology pairs are the most decorrelated.

\subsection{DMD Reveals the Mathematical Signature of Market Efficiency}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_dmd.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{(a)} DMD eigenvalue spectrum (trained 2020--2022, tested 2023--2026).
  All 20 eigenvalues lie well inside the unit circle
  (mean $|\lambda|=0.136$, max $|\lambda|=0.361$), indicating modes that
  decay within a single trading day.
  \textbf{(b)} Lag autocorrelation of daily returns (all 20 stocks pooled).
  The dashed red lines mark the 95\% white-noise confidence interval.
  The slight negative autocorrelation at short lags reflects a known
  microstructure effect (bid-ask bounce) rather than exploitable predictability.}
  \label{fig:dmd}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:dmd}a shows the DMD eigenvalue spectrum.
All 20 eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit circle with mean
$|\lambda| = 0.136$ and maximum $|\lambda| = 0.361$.
This contrasts sharply with systems governed by persistent dynamics—such as
periodic fluid flows—where eigenvalues sit on the unit circle, encoding oscillations
that DMD can faithfully track \citep{kutz2016dynamic}.
Here, every mode decays in well under one trading day: there is no stable
dynamical backbone to extrapolate.

Quantitatively, a DMD model trained on 2020--2022 and evaluated on 2023--2026 achieves
a one-step-ahead RMSE of 0.762 against the zero-prediction baseline of 0.735—
the trained model is \emph{worse} than predicting no change (ratio 1.04).
This is not a failure of DMD per se; it is the correct answer.
In systems with persistent dynamics, DMD succeeds because stable modes carry
predictive power.
In an efficient market, any persistent pattern is rapidly arbitraged away,
leaving only noise.

Figure~\ref{fig:dmd}b shows the lag autocorrelation of returns.
A mild negative autocorrelation at the shortest lags reflects a well-known
microstructure effect (bid-ask bounce, mean-reversion of order-book
imbalances), but the magnitude ($-0.097$ at lag 1) is economically negligible
once transaction costs are considered.
The residual after a rank-3 SVD reconstruction shows no additional
autocorrelation structure beyond the raw returns.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Our analysis resolves what initially appears paradoxical: the market is
simultaneously low-dimensional and unpredictable.
The resolution lies in distinguishing two entirely different questions.

\emph{Cross-sectional structure} (how stocks co-move on any given day) is
low-dimensional, stable, and recoverable: three factors above the random-matrix
noise floor \citep{laloux1999noise}, a clean block-diagonal correlation matrix,
and perfect sector recovery from unlabelled returns.
This structure is the mathematical counterpart of the Fama--French factor model
\citep{fama1993common}.

\emph{Temporal dynamics} (can tomorrow's return be predicted from today's) are
dominated by noise.
DMD eigenvalues near the origin encode modes with sub-day lifetimes; a trained
linear predictor performs worse out-of-sample than the naive zero forecast.
This is the mathematical signature of the Efficient Market Hypothesis
\citep{fama1970efficient}: any learnable temporal pattern is immediately traded
away, leaving only the irreducible noise floor.

SVD, Classical MDS, Spectral Clustering, and DMD together provide a coherent,
multi-angle view of market structure and dynamics: the market is genuinely
low-dimensional across stocks, genuinely noisy across time, and the gap between
the two is precisely where market efficiency lives.
